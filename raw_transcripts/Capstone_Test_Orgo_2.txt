Prompt 2:

Your TA is right to question it. The key issue is the 121% yield, which is impossible for a pure product. Let's check the calculations carefully.
Step 1. Moles of cyclohexene
You reported:
	•	Volume = 2.75 mL
	•	Mass = 2.23 g
Cyclohexene has a density of about 0.811 g/mL.
If you actually used 2.75 mL, then the mass should be:
[ 2.75\ \text{mL} \times 0.811\ \frac{\text{g}}{\text{mL}} = 2.23\ \text{g} ]
So your mass is consistent.
Moles:
[ \frac{2.23\ \text{g}}{82.15\ \text{g/mol}} = 0.0271\ \text{mol} ]
This part is correct.
Step 2. Theoretical yield
Adipic acid molar mass:
[ 146.14\ \text{g/mol} ]
Theoretical mass:
[ 0.0271 \times 146.14 = 3.96\ \text{g} ]
Also correct.
Step 3. Percent yield
[ \frac{4.80}{3.96}\times100 = 121% ]
The math is correct.

So what is actually wrong?
The problem is probably not the calculation—it's the experimental data.
There are a few possibilities:
1. The product wasn't completely dry (most likely)
Adipic acid crystals easily retain:
	•	water from washing
	•	mother liquor
	•	residual acetone or ethanol (depending on the procedure)
Even a few tenths of a gram of retained liquid can push the apparent yield above 100%.
2. Inorganic contamination
Your reaction uses:
	•	sodium tungstate
	•	Aliquat 336
	•	sodium carbonate (in many procedures)
Incomplete washing can leave inorganic salts or catalyst mixed with the crystals.
3. You weighed filter paper or container incorrectly
This is another common cause of yields over 100%.

Your melting point is interesting
You obtained:
151–152 °C
The literature melting point of adipic acid is about 151–153 °C.
A narrow melting range usually indicates fairly pure adipic acid.
That seems inconsistent with a sample that is 21% impurity.
However, there's an important caveat:
Water doesn't always depress the melting point very much if it's simply trapped between crystals rather than incorporated into the crystal lattice. So a wet sample can still melt near the correct temperature.

The spectra
Everything you reported matches adipic acid:
	•	IR broad O–H (2500–3300 cm⁻¹) ✔
	•	IR C=O at 1710 cm⁻¹ ✔
	•	^1H NMR methylene signals ✔
	•	acid proton around 11.5 ppm ✔
So the identity of the product is convincing.

One thing your TA may also expect
Many organic lab manuals ask students to calculate percent yield based on the limiting reagent. Here, hydrogen peroxide is present in excess.
Let's check:
30% H₂O₂ means approximately:
12.0 mL × 1.11 g/mL ≈ 13.3 g solution
30% by mass gives:
≈4.0 g H₂O₂
[ \frac{4.0}{34.01}=0.118\ \text{mol H}_2\text{O}_2 ]
Since 4 mol H₂O₂ are needed per mol cyclohexene:
[ \frac{0.118}{4}=0.0295\ \text{mol} ]
Cyclohexene provides only 0.0271 mol, so cyclohexene is indeed the limiting reagent. Your yield calculation based on cyclohexene is therefore appropriate.
Bottom line
The calculations are correct. The issue is that the experimental yield is physically impossible, so your TA was almost certainly pointing out that the mass of isolated product is too high, not that the arithmetic is wrong.
If this is for a lab report, you should report the calculated 121% yield but explicitly discuss that it indicates the product was not completely dry or contained residual impurities, even though the melting point and spectroscopic data support that the major component is adipic acid. This kind of critical evaluation is usually what instructors are looking for.

Can both still be true?
Yes, if the extra mass isn't another organic compound.
For example:
	•	residual water trapped between crystals
	•	residual mother liquor
	•	traces of sodium salts
	•	residual catalyst
	•	product not fully dried before weighing
These can increase the mass without dramatically affecting the melting point or the NMR if present in modest amounts or if they are not dissolved into the NMR sample.
One thing that caught my attention
